## SECTION 1: Web2 vs Web3 Marketing

Why marketing objectives diverge across Web2 and Web3, and how I've adapted strategy frameworks to work in both ecosystems.
In my experience working across both Web2 and Web3 ecosystems over the past decade, I've found that marketing objectives diverge significantly due to their underlying structures, business models, and user behaviors. Web2 is typically centralized, product-focused, and revenue-driven through subscriptions or ads, while Web3 is decentralized, community-oriented, and often tied to blockchain metrics like user engagement and network effects.
Web2 Marketing Objectives: The Traditional Funnel
I developed my initial framework in the Web2 world, where marketing revolves around a classic sales funnel (often AIDA: Awareness, Interest, Desire, Action) aimed at driving users from discovery to long-term loyalty. The ultimate goal is usually monetizing through a subscription-as-a-service (SaaS) model, where recurring revenue is king.
#### Generating New Traffic and Awareness

This is the top-of-funnel (TOFU) focus. In my experience, marketers use SEO, content marketing, social media ads, influencer partnerships, and PR to attract eyeballs. Metrics here include impressions, website visits, and brand mentions. The aim is broad exposure to build a large audience pool.
#### Nurturing Leads

Once traffic is in, it's about building relationships. Tools like email drip campaigns, webinars, personalized content (e.g., blog posts or ebooks), and retargeting ads keep users engaged. This stage measures engagement rates, open/click-through rates, and lead scoring to qualify prospects.
#### Acquiring and Converting Leads

Mid-funnel (MOFU) efforts push users toward a decision. Tactics include free trials, demos, landing pages with CTAs, A/B testing, and sales outreach. Conversion rates, cost per acquisition (CPA), and lead-to-customer ratios are key KPIs. The focus is on turning interest into sign-ups or purchases.
#### Selling and Closing

Bottom-of-funnel (BOFU) is where the transaction happens. Upselling features, discount codes, testimonials, and urgency tactics (e.g., limited-time offers) drive the sale. For SaaS, this often means securing that initial subscription payment.
#### Retaining Customers

Post-purchase, the emphasis shifts to loyalty and reducing churn. Strategies include onboarding emails, customer success teams, feature updates, loyalty programs, and NPS surveys. Lifetime value (LTV), retention rates, and upsell/cross-sell revenue are tracked to ensure users stick around and spend more.
#### Why This Works in Web2

Centralized platforms control the user experience, data, and monetization. Revenue comes from direct sales or ads, so objectives prioritize scalable, predictable growth.
**Examples I've observed:**

- **Netflix:** Awareness via viral trailers, nurturing through personalized recommendations, conversion with easy sign-ups, retention via content algorithms.

- **HubSpot / Salesforce:** B2B focus on lead gen through inbound marketing, with CRMs tracking the entire funnel.
**Challenges:** High competition for attention, rising ad costs, and privacy regulations (e.g., GDPR) can complicate tracking.
Web3 Marketing Objectives: Ecosystem Growth and On-Chain Metrics
When I transitioned to Web3 marketing, I found that it flips the script—it's less about centralized sales and more about decentralized participation. Projects often rely on tokens, smart contracts, and community governance, so objectives center on network vitality rather than just revenue. Awareness is still foundational, but the real wins come from active, on-chain engagement that boosts token value and ecosystem health.
#### Growing Awareness

Similar to Web2 TOFU, but with a twist—it's often grassroots and viral. In my work, I've used tactics like airdrops, meme marketing, Twitter (X) spaces, Discord AMAs, and collaborations with KOLs (key opinion leaders). Metrics: Social media buzz, token mentions, and community size (e.g., followers or Telegram members). The goal is to educate on the project's utility in a skeptical space.
#### Generating New Daily Active Users (DAUs)

This is the engagement powerhouse. Unlike Web2's passive traffic, Web3 wants users interacting daily via wallets, dApps, or protocols. Strategies I've developed include onboarding incentives like free tokens for sign-ups, gamification (e.g., play-to-earn), referral programs, and integrations with popular chains (e.g., Ethereum or Solana). KPIs: DAU/MAU ratios, wallet connections, and user retention curves.
#### Increasing On-Chain Transaction Volume (Tx Vol)

Here, marketing drives real activity. I've found that campaigns encouraging swaps, staking, or governance votes can spike volume. Tactics: Yield farming promotions, limited-edition NFTs, or DeFi incentives. Metrics: Total value locked (TVL), gas fees generated, and transaction counts. High tx vol signals a thriving network and can pump token prices.
#### Increasing Liquidity

Essential for sustainability, this focuses on deepening pools to reduce slippage and attract traders. Approaches I've used: Liquidity mining rewards, partnerships with DEXs (e.g., Jupiter on Solana), and community-driven events like token burns or AMAs. KPIs: Liquidity depth, trading volume on exchanges, and impermanent loss mitigation.
#### Why This Works in Web3

Decentralization means value accrues to the network, not just the company. Tokens align incentives—users become stakeholders. Marketing often leverages speculation and FOMO (fear of missing out) but must combat rugs and scams.
**Examples I've studied:**

- **Jupiter (Solana DEX aggregator):** Awareness through DeFi community engagement, DAUs via seamless swap UX, tx vol from aggregating the best prices, liquidity via LP partnerships. Their marketing emphasizes speed and Solana's low fees.

- **Hyperliquid (decentralized perp exchange):** Grew DAUs with a focus on serious traders, boosted tx vol through competitive perpetual contracts, liquidity through market maker incentives. Their approach is less meme-heavy, more data-driven.

- **Base (Coinbase's L2):** Marketing emphasizes low-fee transactions to drive adoption and on-chain activity, backed by Coinbase's brand trust.
**Challenges I've navigated:** Volatility, regulatory uncertainty, and "crypto winters" can tank metrics. Plus, metrics are public on-chain, so transparency is a double-edged sword.

Strategic Implications: Blending Web2 and Web3
> 
In my experience, building a strategy for a specific project means thinking about blending elements—e.g., use Web2 funnels to onboard into Web3 ecosystems. The methodology is universal. The examples adapt.
I developed this playbook to work for both ecosystems. Throughout the sections that follow, I present Web3 examples as the primary lens (because that's where I've focused most recently), but I include "Web2 Equivalent" callout boxes to show how the same framework applies to SaaS, B2B, and traditional digital marketing.
**The blended approach I use:**

- **Use Web2 SEO and content funnels** to drive awareness and education about Web3 protocols

- **Apply Web3 community engagement tactics** (Discord, governance, token incentives) to create deeper loyalty

- **Measure both traditional metrics** (conversions, MQLs) **and on-chain metrics** (DAUs, TVL, tx volume)

- **Adapt voice and tone** based on where your audience lives (LinkedIn for enterprise, CT for crypto natives)
The framework is universal. The channels, examples, and metrics shift based on your ecosystem. This playbook gives you both.

## SECTION 2: Content Strategy

I built this framework across Web3 and Web2 projects to nail goals, audience, brand voice, messaging matrix, content funnel, and content pillars that actually drive conversions.
Goals & Objectives
**Primary Goal** — One clear, top-level outcome (e.g. "Grow followers and increase engagement on [channel]," "Generate MQLs from organic content," "Establish thought leadership in [category]"). Everything else supports this.
**Specific Objectives (Time-Bound, Quantified)** — Set 3-month (or quarter) objectives with numbers so you can measure progress and adjust. Base targets on historical data when available.
Example structure:

- Increase [metric A] by X–Y% in 3 months (e.g. smart followers, engagement rate, MQLs).

- Reach [metric B] to [target range] in 3 months (e.g. total engagements, sign-ups from content).
**Baseline / Historical Context** — Document where you are now: current metric values, growth over the last 6–12 months, and why the chosen targets are realistic. Refer back when reporting.
**Template:** Primary goal: [one sentence]. Objectives: (1) [Metric] from [current] to [target] by [date]; (2) [Metric] from [current] to [target]. Baseline: [source and numbers].
Brand Voice & Tone
**Definition** — A short statement of who the brand sounds like (e.g. "Champion of decentralization and self-custody," "Irreverent and bold—like Deadpool," "Mentor, not hero: wise, battle-tested"). This shapes every piece of content.
**Purpose** — Why this voice: cut through noise, differentiate from competitors, build community, make complex topics approachable, spark engagement.
**Core Voice Characteristics** — 3–5 traits with one-line descriptions (e.g. Witty, Bold, Supportive, Pragmatic). Optional: tone scale (e.g. "If Wall Street is 1 and Deadpool is 10, we're 5–7.5").
**Do's and Don'ts** — Do: [e.g. drop pop-culture refs, keep it conversational, sneak in subtle flexes]. Don't: [e.g. go full corporate, use stuffy terms, dry vibes].
**Branded Elements** — Approved emoji, hashtags, or visual cues that stay consistent (e.g. 🟣💜 for a purple brand). Use sparingly but consistently.
Align voice with your Audience Definition and Product Messaging Matrix so tone supports pain points and vision of use.
Audience Definition
> 
You can't build a growth flywheel if you don't know who you're building for or why they can't live without your product. Skip this step and your marketing will be dry, generic, and ineffective.
**The Problem with Vague Audiences:**
"Crypto users" or "B2B SaaS buyers" or "enterprise customers" are too broad to be useful. You need specificity across multiple dimensions to create messaging that resonates.
### Step 1: Define Your Primary Audience

Work through these dimensions to build a crisp, actionable audience definition:
**1. Core Values & Motivations** — What brought them to your product category? What do they care about?
Examples: Web3: Financial freedom vs privacy vs decentralization vs tech experimentation. B2B SaaS: Cost reduction vs innovation leadership vs risk mitigation. Enterprise: Compliance vs competitive advantage vs operational efficiency. Someone motivated by sovereignty needs different messaging than someone motivated by experimentation. This shapes your entire tone.
**2. Success Definition (in context of your product)** — What does winning look like for them? Examples: Crypto native: Financial independence, alpha generation, early access. Startup founder: Product-market fit, fundraising, rapid scaling. Enterprise buyer: Career advancement, risk mitigation, measurable ROI.
**3. Where They Actually Spend Time (Distribution Strategy)** — Your "watering holes" determine where you show up and how you communicate. In my experience working with Web3 protocols, I've found these are the critical channels: Crypto natives: CT (Crypto Twitter), Discord, Telegram, Farcaster, governance forums. DeFi traders: Crypto Twitter, protocol Discords, Dune Analytics, DeFiLlama. Infrastructure builders: GitHub, protocol forums, technical documentation sites, Discord dev channels. Your content and partnerships should meet them where they already are, not where you wish they were.
**[WEB2 EQUIVALENT]**
**Web2 Distribution Channels:** Technical builders: GitHub, Stack Overflow, Reddit, Hacker News. Enterprise buyers: LinkedIn, industry conferences, analyst reports (Gartner, Forrester). Startup founders: Product Hunt, Indie Hackers, YC community, LinkedIn. B2B SaaS buyers: G2, Capterra, industry Slack communities, webinars.
**4. What They Care About Beyond Your Category** — The ecosystems they follow, cultural trends they track, other products/communities they support. This context helps you speak their language naturally, find partnership opportunities that feel authentic, create content that references their world, and build positioning that resonates culturally.

**Multiple Audience Segments:** Most products serve 2-4 distinct audience segments. Create a separate definition for each.
**Prioritization:** (1) Which segment has the highest conversion rate? (2) Which has the highest lifetime value? (3) Which is easiest to reach (distribution advantage)? (4) Which provides best word-of-mouth/viral growth? Focus 70% of effort on your #1 segment, 30% on #2. Ignore #3+ until you've saturated the first two.
**Context-Dependent Messaging:** I've learned that the same audience might need different messaging based on market conditions. In Web3, this is critical:
**Bull Market vs Bear Market (Crypto/Web3):** Bull = speed, opportunity capture, FOMO prevention ("Don't miss the next 100x"), aggressive calls to action. Bear = safety, sustainability, long-term value ("Build during the bear"), focus on fundamentals and utility over speculation. Jupiter adapted their messaging from "fastest swaps" (bull) to "most reliable liquidity" (bear).
**[WEB2 EQUIVALENT]**
**Web2 Market Context Examples:**
**Early Stage vs Growth Stage (B2B SaaS):** Early = flexibility, speed to value, low commitment, month-to-month pricing; Growth = scalability, integration with existing stack, enterprise features, annual contracts.
**Market Leader vs Challenger (Enterprise):** Leader = stability, ecosystem, proven results, "nobody gets fired for choosing us"; Challenger = innovation, cost savings, agility, "move faster than your competition".
Your product messaging matrix (below) should be adapted for these contexts, not built as a single static document.

```
PAIN POINT → CAPABILITY → FEATURE FUNCTION → VISION OF USE → BENEFIT
```
Start with pain, not features. Focus on your top 3 differentiated features so you can measure what messaging actually performs.

Writing guidelines

- **Use "you" language** — Be the user. "You're stuck waiting..." not "Users experience friction."

- **Make pain vivid** — Bad: "Gas management creates inefficiency." Good: "You spot a play on Solana but you don't have SOL for gas. You're stuck swapping, bridging. The opportunity slips by. Again."

- **Vision of use = cinematic** — Show the feature in action with specific, emotional detail; don't just explain it.
#### Example: Unified Gas Tank (Web3)

**Pain Point:** You spot a play on Solana but you don't have any SOL for gas. You're stuck swapping, bridging, waiting. The opportunity slips by while you're scrambling for the right token. Again.
**Capability:** Move across chains instantly without thinking about gas logistics.
**Feature Function:** Deposit USDC once into a universal gas tank that covers transactions across 36 blockchains automatically.
**Vision of Use:** You're bridging from Ethereum to Solana and the move is happening NOW. No time to source gas. You execute immediately. Your timing is perfect and you watch half of CT scrambling on your timeline.
**Benefit:** Never miss a time-sensitive opportunity because of gas management again.
#### Example: Best Price Aggregation (Jupiter, Solana DEX)

**Pain Point:** You're swapping on a single DEX and getting slippage that eats your gains. You know there are better prices out there across multiple pools, but checking them all manually is impossible. You're leaving money on the table with every trade.
**Capability:** Get the best possible swap price across all Solana DEXs instantly without the manual work.
**Feature Function:** Smart routing algorithm that checks prices across all major Solana DEXs (Orca, Raydium, Serum) and automatically splits your order for optimal execution.
**Vision of Use:** You're trading volatile assets and every basis point matters. You execute a large SOL-to-USDC swap. Jupiter routes it across three pools simultaneously, saving you 0.8% compared to a single DEX. That's $800 on a $100k trade—instant alpha.
**Benefit:** Maximize returns on every trade without manual price checking or complex routing logic.
**[WEB2 EQUIVALENT]**
**B2B SaaS Example: CRM Contact Enrichment**
**Pain Point:** Your sales team is manually researching prospects on LinkedIn, company websites, and scattered databases before every call. It takes 15-20 minutes per lead and the data is often outdated. They're spending more time researching than selling.
**Capability:** Automatically enrich every contact with current firmographic, technographic, and intent data the moment they enter your CRM.
**Feature Function:** Real-time API integrations with data providers (Clearbit, ZoomInfo, etc.) that populate contact records automatically with company size, tech stack, recent funding, and buying signals.
**Vision of Use:** A hot lead fills out your demo form at 9 AM. By 9:02 AM, your AE sees their complete profile: Series B, 200 employees, using Salesforce, recently posted a job for a sales ops manager. Your AE personalizes the outreach in seconds and books the call while the lead is still engaged.
**Benefit:** Sales team spends 80% less time on research and 3x more time having qualified conversations.
Same structure applies across any category—pain first, then capability and scenario.
Where it gets used

- **Social:** Post 1 = pain (hook); 2–3 = capability/function; 4 = vision of use (close).

- **Landing pages:** Headline = pain; subhead = capability; body = function; close = vision of use; CTA = benefit.

- **Content briefs:** Pain as hook, vision of use as examples, benefit as CTA.

- **Sales:** Pain opens discovery; capability qualifies; vision of use closes.
Track what resonates (which pain points, which visions get shared) and iterate the matrix from real feedback.
Content Funnel Map
**Purpose** — Map content types and goals to each stage of the buyer journey so every asset has a clear role and success metric. Use this to plan campaigns, assign KPIs, and avoid creating content that doesn't move people toward conversion.

- **Awareness (TOFU)** — Audience doesn't know they have a problem or your category yet. Goal: reach, brand recall, education.

- **Consideration (MOFU)** — They know the problem and are evaluating options. Goal: consideration set inclusion, trust, differentiation.

- **Decision (BOFU)** — Ready to choose. Goal: conversion, sign-up, demo, purchase.

- **Retention & Advocacy** — Existing users. Goal: retention, expansion, referrals, UGC.

Content Pillar Architecture
**Overview:** Content pillars organize your content into strategic clusters that drive conversions, not just topical coverage. The key is structuring clusters by buying intent, not just subject matter.
**Hub & Spoke Model:** Hub Page = comprehensive guide targeting primary keyword. Spoke Pages = supporting content targeting related keywords. Internal Linking = systematic cross-linking between hub and spoke. Conversion Path = CTAs placed strategically throughout cluster.

## SECTION 3: Pain Point SEO

I developed this three-tier keyword model after seeing too many teams chase traffic over conversions. It works across Web3 and Web2—the buying intent signals are universal.
Strategic Shift: Conversions Over Traffic
**Traditional SEO Mistake:** Most content teams prioritize keywords by: (1) Search volume (higher = better), (2) Keyword difficulty (lower = better). Result: High traffic, low conversions (0.03–0.19% typical).
**Pain Point SEO Approach:** Prioritize keywords by: (1) Buying intent (higher = better), (2) Commercial value (higher = better), (3) Ranking difficulty (secondary consideration). Result: Lower traffic, high conversions (0.3–4.3% typical).
> 
A keyword with 100 searches/month and 2% conversion rate generates more value than a keyword with 10,000 searches/month and 0.05% conversion rate.
**Calculation:** 100 searches × 30% CTR × 2% conversion = 0.6 conversions/month. 10,000 searches × 30% CTR × 0.05% conversion = 1.5 conversions/month. The high-volume keyword wins by 2.5x, but requires 100x more traffic. ROI per content piece heavily favors the intent-focused approach.
Tier 1: Category Keywords (Extreme Bottom of Funnel)
People searching these terms are ready to buy. They don't need education or nurturing.
**Characteristics:** Direct product/service category searches; searcher already knows the solution type; high commercial intent; often high competition but worth fighting for.
**Examples I've targeted [DeFi/Web3]:** "best Solana DEX", "Solana DEX aggregator", "decentralized perp exchange", "best decentralized derivatives platform", "Solana swap platform", "on-chain perpetual futures".
**Examples [GPU/Infra]:** "decentralized GPU platform", "GPU cloud computing service", "distributed GPU infrastructure", "bare metal GPU hosting", "decentralized AI compute".
**Content Formats:** Product/service landing pages, feature comparison pages, pricing pages optimized for these terms, "Best [category]" listicles that position your product.
**[WEB2 EQUIVALENT]**
**Web2 Category Keywords (B2B SaaS):** "customer data platform", "marketing automation software", "sales engagement platform", "project management tool for agencies", "accounting software for startups".
**Research Process:** (1) List all ways someone could describe your product category. (2) Ask sales team: "What do prospects search for before finding us?" (3) Ask customers: "If you were to research our product, what would you search for?" (4) Check competitor title tags and H1s for category positioning. (5) Use DataForSEO to find variations and search volumes.
Tier 2: Comparison & Alternative Keywords (Extreme Bottom of Funnel)
People searching these are comparing options or looking to switch. They're deep in the buying process.
**Characteristics:** Indicates active product evaluation; often underestimated search volume (tools show zero, reality differs); lower competition than category keywords; very high conversion rates.
**Examples I've used [DeFi/Web3]:** "Jupiter vs Raydium", "Hyperliquid vs dYdX", "best Hyperliquid alternatives", "Jupiter vs 1inch comparison", "dYdX alternatives for perpetual futures", "Raydium vs Orca swap comparison", "how to reduce swap slippage on Solana".
**Examples [GPU/Infra]:** "AWS GPU pricing vs [Your Product]", "CoreWeave alternatives", "Lambda Labs vs [Your Product]", "Paperspace alternatives for AI training", "RunPod vs [Your Product] comparison".
**Content Formats:** Head-to-head comparison articles, "X Alternatives" listicles, feature comparison tables, migration guides from competitors.
**[WEB2 EQUIVALENT]**
**Web2 Comparison Keywords (B2B SaaS):** "HubSpot vs Salesforce", "Marketo alternatives", "best alternatives to Asana", "Slack vs Microsoft Teams for enterprise", "Figma vs Adobe XD comparison", "migrating from Intercom to [Your Product]".
**Research Process:** (1) Identify your top 5-10 competitors. (2) Create comparison keyword list for each: "[Competitor] vs [You]", "[You] vs [Competitor]", "[Competitor] alternative", "Best [Competitor] alternatives", "[Competitor] competitors". (3) Don't be discouraged by "zero search volume" in tools. (4) Write the content anyway if the competitor has significant market share. (5) Track actual traffic in GSC to validate.
See also: Advanced: "Zero Volume" Keyword Strategy below.
Tier 3: Jobs-to-be-Done Keywords (Mid-Funnel)
These keywords indicate someone has a problem your product solves, even if they're not explicitly searching for your solution type yet.
**Characteristics:** Problem-aware, not necessarily solution-aware; still have buying intent (some fraction will purchase); often "how to" queries; medium competition; higher volume than Tier 1 & 2, lower conversion than Tier 1 & 2.
**Examples I've targeted [DeFi/Web3]:** "how to reduce swap slippage on Solana", "how to find best DEX prices automatically", "how to trade perpetual futures on-chain", "how to avoid front-running on DEX swaps", "how to maximize liquidity provider returns", "how to get started with DeFi trading".
**Examples [GPU/Infra]:** "how to reduce GPU training costs", "how to scale AI inference workloads", "how to migrate from AWS to decentralized GPU", "how to manage distributed GPU clusters", "how to optimize GPU utilization for AI".
**Content Formats:** How-to guides that present your product as a solution, problem-solution tutorials, technical implementation guides, best practices articles.
**[WEB2 EQUIVALENT]**
**Web2 JTBD Keywords (B2B SaaS):** "how to improve sales team productivity", "how to automate lead scoring", "how to reduce customer churn", "how to build a content marketing funnel", "how to integrate CRM with marketing automation".
**Research Process:** (1) Interview customers: "What problem were you trying to solve before finding us?" (2) Interview sales: "What do prospects say they need help with?" (3) Extract "jobs to be done" from customer conversations. (4) Prefix with "how to" / "ways to" / "best practices for". (5) Validate that your product legitimately solves this problem. (6) Ensure content provides value beyond just pitching your product.
**Critical Rule for JTBD Content:** Your product should be presented as one solution among several, not the only solution. Provide genuine value first, conversion opportunity second. This builds trust and improves conversion rates.
Customer Interview Questions for Keyword Discovery
**Question Set 1: Category Keywords** (ask customers and sales team):

- "How would you describe our product/service to someone who knew nothing about us?" — Listen for category descriptors: "It's a decentralized GPU platform" → category keyword.

- "If you were to research our product, what would you Google?" — Exact phrases become target keywords.

- "What words would you use to explain what we do to a technical colleague?" — Technical audience may use different terms than marketing assumes.
**Question Set 2: Comparison/Alternative Keywords** (ask customers):

- "If our product no longer existed, what would you use instead?" — These are your true competitors → build comparison content.

- "What other products did you evaluate before choosing us?" — Build "[Competitor] vs [Us]" content for each.

- "What product were you using before switching to us?" — Build "[Old Product] alternative" content.
**Question Set 3: Jobs-to-be-Done Keywords** (ask customers):

- "What specific problem were you trying to solve when you found us?" — Turn problem statements into "how to [solve problem]" keywords.

- "What are the top 3 benefits you get from our product?" — Each benefit suggests a problem → keyword opportunity.

- "What became possible after using our product that wasn't before?" — Capabilities unlocked = problems solved = keywords.
**Question Set 4: Search Behavior** (ask sales team):

- "What questions do prospects ask on discovery calls?" — Common questions = search queries.

- "What do prospects say they've already tried before talking to us?" — Failed solutions indicate problem awareness → JTBD keywords.

- "What objections do you hear most often?" — Objections reveal concerns → create content addressing them.
**Question Set 5: Web3-Specific Discovery** (ask customers):

- "Which chains or L2s are you most active on?" — Chain-specific keywords (Solana, Base, Arbitrum).

- "Where do you go to find alpha or research protocols?" — Distribution channels for content (CT, Discord, Dune).

- "What metrics do you track most closely?" — TVL, APY, transaction volume, slippage → keyword opportunities.

- "Which DEXs or protocols have you switched away from, and why?" — Competitor comparison content gold.

**Implementation:** Conduct 10-15 customer interviews; survey 20-30 customers with open-ended questions; interview entire sales or BD team; look for repeated phrases (5+ people saying same thing); exact customer language often differs from how marketing describes the product.
Competitor Analysis Process (Pain Point SEO Lens)
**Phase 1: Identify Who's Actually Winning Conversions** — Don't just analyze competitors with high traffic. Find competitors who are likely converting well. Indicators: Strong presence on comparison keywords; high CPC values on their brand terms (indicates commercial value); robust category keyword positioning; case studies and ROI calculators prominent on site; PPC spend on bottom-of-funnel terms.
**Phase 2: Reverse-Engineer Their Conversion Keywords** — For top 3-5 competitors: (1) Extract all ranked keywords via DataForSEO. (2) Filter for: comparison keywords (contains "vs", "alternative", "comparison"), category keywords (contains your product category terms), JTBD keywords (contains "how to", "ways to", "best practices"). (3) Ignore informational keywords unless strategic. (4) Check which keywords they're running PPC for (indicates conversion value).
**Phase 3: Identify Comparison Gaps** — Critical opportunity: competitors rarely create "Competitor vs Us" pages because it feels awkward. For each major competitor, check if they have "[Their Brand] vs [Other Competitor]" or "Best alternatives to [Other Competitor]" content. Create these pages from your perspective; position yourself favorably. Often ranks easily because competitor won't create counter-content. Example: If Competitor A and B don't have "A vs B" content, you create "Competitor A vs Competitor B vs [Your Product]" and capture that search traffic.
**Phase 4: CPC-Weighted Opportunity Scoring**
```
Opportunity Score = (Search Volume / Keyword Difficulty) × CPC × Intent Tier Multiplier
Intent Tier Multipliers:

- Category Keywords: 3.0

- Comparison/Alternative: 3.0

- JTBD Keywords: 2.0

- Informational: 0.5
```
This formula systematically deprioritizes high-volume, low-intent keywords that traditional SEO would chase.
Keyword Research Workflow (Pain Point SEO Method)
**Step 1: Start with Customer Language, Not Tools** — Complete customer/sales interviews; extract exact phrases customers use; document problems they were trying to solve; list all mentioned competitors/alternatives.
**Step 2: Build Category Keyword List** — Start with how customers describe your product; add category synonyms; include modifiers (best, top, leading, enterprise, etc.); add location modifiers if relevant; include technology stack modifiers (cloud, decentralized, etc.).
**Step 3: Build Comparison Keyword List** — List all competitors mentioned by customers. For each competitor create: "[You] vs [Competitor]", "[Competitor] vs [You]", "[Competitor] alternative", "[Competitor] alternatives", "Best alternatives to [Competitor]", "[Competitor] competitors". Don't filter by search volume yet.
**Step 4: Build JTBD Keyword List** — Take each problem statement from interviews; convert to "how to [solve problem]", "ways to [solve problem]", "best practices for [solving problem]", "[problem] solution"; validate your product genuinely solves this problem; only keep keywords where you can provide real value.
**Step 5: Validate with DataForSEO** — Use google_keyword_ideas for related terms and volume; google_ads_search_volume to validate actual volume; bulk_keyword_difficulty to assess ranking difficulty; extract CPC data for commercial intent.
**Step 6: Calculate Pain Point SEO Scores** — For each keyword: Score = (Volume / Difficulty) × CPC × Intent Multiplier. Sort by: (1) Tier 1 & 2 by score, (2) Tier 3 by score, (3) Archive informational unless strategic.
**Step 7: Prioritize Based on Resource Constraints**

- **1-2 writers:** Focus exclusively on Tier 1 & 2 for 6-12 months; only move to Tier 3 after exhausting bottom-of-funnel opportunities.

- **3-5 writers:** 70% effort on Tier 1 & 2, 30% on highest-scoring Tier 3.

- **6+ writers:** 50% Tier 1 & 2, 40% Tier 3, 10% strategic top-of-funnel (linkbait, brand awareness).
**Critical Insight:** Most content teams are over-resourced for top-of-funnel and under-resourced for bottom-of-funnel. Flip this ratio.

Measuring Success: Conversions, Not Traffic
**Critical shift in metrics:** Traditional SEO tracks traffic, rankings, and domain authority. Pain Point SEO tracks conversions by article, conversion rate by keyword tier, and revenue influenced by content.
**What to track in Google Analytics:** Set up events for product signups from blog posts, demo requests from specific articles, free trial starts, and contact form submissions from content. Use last-click (and optionally first-click) attribution.
**Key insight:** The article ranked 16th for traffic can generate the most conversions. Prioritize by conversion rank, not traffic rank.
Optimization:

- **High traffic + low conversions:** Improve CTAs; add product mentions; consider archiving if top-of-funnel.

- **Low traffic + high conversions:** Improve content to rank higher; build cluster; double down on this tier.

- **High traffic + high conversions:** Maintain; use as link destination; replicate format.

- **Low traffic + low conversions:** Deprioritize or archive; redirect to stronger content.
Advanced: "Zero Volume" Keyword Strategy
**The Pattern:** Tools like Ahrefs, SEMrush, and Moz frequently show zero search volume for keywords that actually drive conversions.
**Why This Happens:** (1) Long-tail combinations not in tool databases. (2) Emerging search patterns not yet captured. (3) B2B searches with small absolute volume but high value. (4) Localized or industry-specific terminology. (5) **Web3-specific:** Crypto terminology moves faster than SEO tools can index—new protocols, meme coins, and trading slang emerge weekly.
How to Identify Valuable Zero-Volume Keywords:
**Method 1: Customer Language Audit** — Transcribe 10 sales calls; extract exact questions prospects ask; turn questions into keywords; write content even if tools show zero volume.
**Method 2: Competitor Analysis** — Find competitor content with no keyword ranking in tools; check if it gets backlinks (signals value); check if they're running PPC to it (signals conversions); create competing content.
**Method 3: Search Suggestion Mining** — Type seed keywords into Google; review "People Also Ask" and "Related Searches"; capture autocomplete suggestions; many won't show volume in tools but get actual searches.
**Method 4: Forum and Community Mining** — Find industry communities (Reddit, Discord, Slack, Crypto Twitter, Telegram, Farcaster); extract common questions/problems; turn into keywords; create content addressing them. In Web3: monitor protocol Discords, governance forums, and CT threads for emerging pain points.
**[WEB2 EQUIVALENT]**
**Web2-Specific Mining:** Industry forums (Product Hunt, Indie Hackers, niche Subreddits), Slack/Discord communities, G2 and Capterra reviews for competitor pain points.
**Validation Process:** (1) Create content targeting "zero volume" keyword. (2) Track in Google Search Console for 90 days. (3) Measure actual impressions and clicks. (4) If receives traffic: tool was wrong, keyword is valuable. (5) If no traffic after 90 days: keyword genuinely has no volume.
**Success Rate:** Approximately 30% of "zero volume" keywords actually drive meaningful traffic and conversions. Worth the experimentation investment.

## SECTION 5: Case Study Development

I developed this process to get customers to participate, run conversion-focused interviews, and structure case studies that support SEO and close deals. Works across Web3 protocols and Web2 SaaS—the structure is universal.
Anatomy of a Case Study
Effective case studies follow a consistent shape: an impact card above the fold, then Challenge → Solution & Implementation → Results, with optional technical details and takeaways. This structure matches what converts (proof, numbers, quotes) and what supports content (Tier 1/2/3 keywords).
Standard structure:

- **Impact summary card (above fold)** — One-line challenge; 3–5 key results (cost %, time saved, scale); hero quote; CTA. Readers should get the story in 30 seconds.

- **The challenge** — About the company (stage, size, what they do). What they were struggling with (cost, performance, procurement, business impact). Why they needed a change. Optional: what they tried before.

- **The solution** — Why they chose you (decision factors, decisive moment). What they implemented (products, deployment method, timeline). Pull quote on implementation or technical advantage.

- **The results** — By the numbers (before/after table when relevant; cost comparison table for cost-focused stories). Business/strategic impact. What became possible. Second hero quote.

- **Optional:** Technical details (architecture, integration, security). Key takeaways (what worked, lessons, advice for others).

- **CTA** — Try product, contact sales, or read related content.
Use a cost comparison table (e.g. Provider | Total cost | vs baseline) when the story is cost-focused; it reinforces the key result and is highly scannable.
Getting Customers to Participate
**The challenge:** Case studies are your strongest conversion content, but customers are busy, cautious about sharing competitive intel, or don't see the value. Strategy and incentives matter.
Customer identification:
**Tier 1 (pursue actively):** Impressive, quantifiable results; recognizable brands in your target market; customers who switched from a specific competitor; vocal advocates.
**Tier 2 (opportunistic):** Satisfied customers with good (not spectacular) results; smaller companies willing to trade exposure for credibility; emerging use cases you want to expand into.
**Tier 3 (strategic):** Enterprise logos for new verticals; use cases that match your highest-value keywords; willingness to do video.
**Outreach timing:** Best: 60–90 days after successful implementation. Good: after a support win, or at renewal. Avoid: during onboarding, after complaints, or their busy seasons.

```

Worth a conversation?

```

Monetary (credits, extended terms, discount). Exposure (webinar, guest post, social spotlight, press intro). Relationship (product access, beta, executive events). Match to company stage—startups often value exposure; enterprises often prefer relationship perks.

I built this reporting framework after seeing too many teams drown in vanity metrics. Track what drives conversions, revenue, and protocol health—not just traffic.

Green/Yellow/Orange/Red Zone System
**What I use:** A simple color-coding system to quickly assess content performance. Traffic is NOT the primary input—conversion rate and business impact are.
Zone Definitions:
**🟢 Green Zone (Winning Content):**

- Conversion rate &gt;2% (Tier 1/2) or &gt;0.5% (Tier 3)

- Ranking in top 5 for target keyword

- Traffic stable or growing month-over-month

- Revenue/TVL influence high relative to other content
Action: Maintain. Build cluster around it. Replicate format for similar keywords. Use as internal link destination.
**🟡 Yellow Zone (Needs Optimization):**

- Conversion rate 0.5–2% (Tier 1/2) or 0.1–0.5% (Tier 3)

- Ranking #6–15 for target keyword (page 1–2)

- Traffic flat or slightly declining
Action: Optimize CTAs, add more proof elements, improve content to rank higher, build cluster to support it. Review and improve within 30 days.
**🟠 Orange Zone (Underperforming):**

- Conversion rate &lt;0.5% (Tier 1/2) or &lt;0.1% (Tier 3)

- Ranking #16+ (page 2+)

- Low or declining traffic
Action: Decide—major rewrite to salvage, or redirect to stronger content and deprioritize. If Tier 1/2, prioritize fixing (high potential). If Tier 3, consider archiving.
**🔴 Red Zone (Archive or Redirect):**

- Zero or near-zero conversions over 90 days

- No meaningful traffic

- Keyword has no search volume (validated via GSC)
Action: Redirect to related content (301 redirect). Archive or delete. Don't waste maintenance effort on dead content.
Quarterly Content Audit Using Zones:
Every quarter, tag every published article with a zone color. Create action plan: 70% effort maintaining/expanding Green Zone, 25% optimizing Yellow, 5% deciding on Orange/Red.
**[WEB2 EQUIVALENT]**
**Web2 B2B Zone Tweaks:** Green = 5% MQL conversion (Tier 1/2), 1% (Tier 3). Yellow = 2–5% (Tier 1/2). Measure pipeline $ influenced instead of just conversions—higher impact visibility to leadership.
Content Success Metrics (4 Categories)
I organize success metrics into four buckets: SEO Performance, Engagement, Business Impact, and Efficiency. Track all four to get a complete picture.
1. SEO Performance Metrics

- **Organic Traffic:** Sessions from organic search (Google, Bing). Track by article, by cluster, by tier.

- **Keyword Rankings:** Position for target keywords. Track weekly or monthly. Focus on Tier 1/2 keywords.

- **Impressions (GSC):** How many times your content appeared in search results. Leading indicator of traffic.

- **Click-Through Rate (GSC):** Impressions → clicks. Low CTR = bad title/meta description. Optimize.

- **Backlinks:** Number of referring domains linking to your content. Quality quantity.
2. Engagement Metrics

- **Time on Page:** Are readers actually reading? 1 min = bounce. 3 min = engaged.

- **Scroll Depth:** What % of readers scroll to bottom? Tracks content quality and CTA placement effectiveness.

- **Bounce Rate:** % of single-page sessions. High bounce on Tier 1/2 = problem (wrong audience or bad content).

- **Internal Click-Through:** Did they click internal links to other content or product pages? Measures funnel progression.
3. Business Impact Metrics (Most Important)

- **Conversions:** Wallet connects, sign-ups, trials, demo requests—whatever your conversion goal is.

- **Conversion Rate:** (Conversions / Sessions) × 100. The single best metric for content quality.

- **Revenue/TVL Influenced:** How much business value did this content drive? Use attribution modeling.

- **Customer LTV by Source:** Do content-acquired users stay longer or spend more? (Web2-specific, but trackable on-chain for Web3.)

**[WEB2 EQUIVALENT]**
**Web2 Business Impact Examples:** MQLs generated: 87. Pipeline influenced: $430K. Closed-won revenue: $68K. Customer LTV (cohort): $12,400. Payback period: 4 months (content cost / revenue influenced).
4. Efficiency Metrics (Operational)

- **Cost per Article:** Writer time + strategist time + any external costs (tools, design). Track to manage budget.

- **Time to Publish:** Brief → draft → review → publish. Optimize bottlenecks.

- **Cost per Conversion:** (Total content cost / Total conversions). Compare to paid acquisition cost (PPC, etc.).

- **ROI:** (Revenue influenced / Content cost) × 100. Content ROI often beats paid channels 5–10x.

**Audience:** Content team (strategist, writers, social).
**Metrics:**

- Articles published this week (by tier)

- Articles in draft/review/stuck

- Ranking changes for top 10 target keywords

Monthly Reporting (Performance Review)

- Conversions (total, by tier, top 10 converting articles)

- Ranking improvements (new page 1 rankings, lost rankings)

- Revenue/TVL influenced (if attribution is set up)
**Format:** Shared doc or dashboard. 1-page summary + detailed data appendix. 30-min review meeting.
Quarterly Reporting (Strategic)
**Audience:** Marketing lead, executive team, board (if relevant).
**Metrics:**

Attribution Models (Last-Touch vs Multi-Touch)
**The challenge:** Users rarely convert on first visit. They read multiple articles, visit from different channels, research over days or weeks. Which content gets credit?
Attribution Models I've Used:
**1. Last-Touch Attribution (Simplest)**
Give 100% credit to the last piece of content before conversion. Easy to implement and understand. Downside: ignores earlier touchpoints that built trust.
**When to use:** Small team, limited analytics setup, need quick wins. Better than no attribution.
**2. First-Touch Attribution**
Give 100% credit to the first piece of content that brought user in. Shows which content is best at attracting new users. Downside: ignores nurture content that closed the deal.
**When to use:** Alongside last-touch for comparison. Helps identify top-of-funnel winners.
**3. Linear Multi-Touch**
Give equal credit to every content touchpoint in the conversion path. More fair but harder to track (requires cookie/wallet tracking across sessions).
**When to use:** Mature content operation, GA4 or Mixpanel tracking set up, want to understand full funnel.
**4. Position-Based (U-Shaped)**
Give 40% credit to first touch, 40% to last touch, 20% split among middle touchpoints. Recognizes both discovery and closing content.
**When to use:** My preferred model for Web3 and Web2. Balances simplicity with accuracy.
Implementation:
**Web3 (Wallet-Based Tracking):**

- User visits "Jupiter vs Raydium" → cookie or UTM param stores visit

- User returns to "How to Swap on Jupiter" → second touchpoint tracked

- User connects wallet → conversion event tied to wallet address + touchpoint history

- Track wallet's subsequent on-chain behavior (swaps, volume, retention) → attribute back to content
Tool: Google Analytics 4, Mixpanel, Amplitude, or custom on-chain analytics (Dune, Flipside).
**[WEB2 EQUIVALENT]**
**Web2 B2B Attribution:** Track form fills, demo requests, trial sign-ups tied to cookie ID. Sync with CRM (HubSpot, Salesforce) to follow lead → opportunity → closed-won. Multi-touch models show which content assists vs closes. Marketing ops often owns attribution model and reporting. Tool: HubSpot, Marketo, Bizible, Dreamdata.
**Practical Advice:** Start with last-touch. It's better than guessing. Upgrade to position-based when you have 6+ months of data and can invest in tracking setup.

## SECTION 9: Team Collaboration Patterns

I built these systems and workflows working across Web3 protocols and Web2 SaaS teams. The roles adapt (add DevRel, Community Manager for Web3), but the flow is universal.
How It All Fits Together
Everything in Sections 1–5 runs in sequence. Strategy sets the inputs; keywords and clusters feed the calendar; briefs and case studies feed production; social repurposes and engages. Define ownership and cadence at each step so nothing sits in a silo.
End-to-end flow:

- **Content Strategy (Section 1)** — Goals, audience, voice, messaging matrix, funnel map, pillars. Owner: strategist. Cadence: set at kickoff; revisit quarterly.

- **Pain Point SEO (Section 2)** — Keyword research → tiered backlog, cluster plan, prioritization by resource. Owner: strategist (or dedicated SEO). Cadence: backlog refresh monthly; cluster phasing per plan (e.g. Months 1–3 category, 4–6 competitor, 7–9 JTBD).

- **Content calendar** — Assigns keywords/clusters to briefs and publish dates. Owner: strategist. Cadence: weekly review; briefs due 1–2 weeks before writer start.

- **Content Production (Section 3)** — Brief → writer → draft → review → publish. Owner: writer executes; strategist owns brief and review. Cadence: per your editorial calendar (e.g. 2–4 articles/month).

- **Case Study Development (Section 4)** — Outreach → interview → write → approve → publish. Owner: strategist or dedicated PM for outreach; writer for draft. Cadence: pipeline review monthly; completed case studies feed proof library for briefs.

- **Social Media (Section 5)** — Content mix, schedule, engagement, per-stage plan. Owner: one person (or rotation). Cadence: batch weekly/biweekly; repurpose every new Tier 1/2 article and case study into threads/posts; reply SLA per playbook.
Keep a single source of truth for: keyword backlog (tier, cluster, status), content calendar (brief → draft → review → publish), case study pipeline (outreach → interview → draft → approval), and proof asset library (case studies, quotes, metrics by use case/competitor).
Roles & Responsibilities
**Strategist (or strategy + SEO)**

- Owns Content Strategy (Section 1) and Pain Point SEO (Section 2): goals, audience, messaging matrix, keyword research, tier classification, cluster plan.

- Produces content briefs (Section 3 template): keyword, intent, conversion strategy, CTAs, proof elements, outline.

- Owns content calendar: assigns keywords to briefs, deadlines, handoff to writer.

- Reviews drafts: conversion strategy implemented, CTAs in place, proof included, voice/tone.

- Often owns or coordinates case study outreach and interview scheduling; may hand off interview notes to writer for draft.
**Writer**

- Executes conversion-focused brief: outline, positioning, CTAs, proof elements.

- Writes for the buyer, not just the searcher; weaves product positioning per tier (aggressive for Tier 1/2, balanced for Tier 3).

- Finds or requests proof assets (case study links, quotes, data) when brief specifies them.

- May own case study drafts from interview notes; may own first-pass social repurposing (threads from blog posts).
**Social / engagement owner**

- Owns content mix, posting schedule, and reply/engagement playbook (Section 5).

- Repurposes new blog and case study content into threads, posts, and clips per cadence.

- Responsible for reply SLA and escalation; proactive engagement in community.
**Web3-Specific Roles (add to above):**

- **Community Manager:** Owns Discord, Telegram moderation; answers technical questions; escalates bugs/exploits; weekly community sentiment report to team. Often coordinates with social owner on CT replies and Spaces.

- **DevRel (Developer Relations):** Writes technical documentation, sample code, integration guides; speaks at conferences and hackathons; publishes dev-focused blog content (often bypasses standard brief process for speed). Bridges product/eng and marketing.

- **Tokenomics/On-Chain Analyst:** Provides data for case studies and content (TVL, tx volume, user cohorts from Dune/Flipside); validates claims in marketing content; creates dashboards content can link to. Not always marketing-org but collaborates closely.
**[WEB2 EQUIVALENT]**
**Web2-Specific Roles:** SDR/BDR teams (prospecting, qualifying MQLs from content), Product Marketing (positioning, launches, competitive intel, sales enablement), Analyst Relations (Gartner, Forrester briefings), Demand Gen (paid campaigns, ABM, webinars), Marketing Ops (CRM, attribution, reporting automation).
**Feedback loop:** Review conversion data monthly (conversions by article, by tier); share what's working; adjust CTA copy, proof elements, and prioritization. Evolve briefs and playbook from real results.
Briefing & Handoff
**What the strategist provides before the writer starts:**

- Full brief using the Pain Point SEO brief template (Section 3): keyword, tier, intent analysis, conversion strategy, CTAs, proof elements, outline, internal linking.

- Access to: messaging matrix, brand voice doc, audience definition (so writer can match tone and pain points).

- Proof asset list with links or placeholders: which case studies, quotes, or data to include; if not yet created, "TBD from [upcoming case study]" or "use [metric] from [source]".
**Handoff sequence:**

- Keyword backlog (Section 2) → strategist prioritizes and assigns next keywords to briefs.

- Brief completed → assign to writer with due date; writer confirms access to strategy docs and proof assets.

- Writer delivers draft → strategist reviews (see Review & Approval); writer revises; repeat until approved.

- Approved draft → publish; then trigger repurposing (social) and internal link updates (other articles linking to this one).
**Timing:** Brief due 1–2 weeks before writer start date so writer can clarify questions. Avoid "brief and draft due same week"—writer needs time to research and request proof assets.
Review & Approval Workflow
**Stages:** (1) Outline review (optional but recommended for Tier 1/2)—strategist checks conversion logic and CTA placement before full draft. (2) Draft review—strategist (and optionally stakeholder) reviews against brief and checklist.
**Who reviews:** Strategist always. For Tier 1/2 or high-visibility pieces, add one stakeholder (product, marketing lead) for a single round; avoid design-by-committee.
**Criteria:** Conversion strategy implemented; CTAs in place and contextual; proof elements included; product positioning matches tier; voice/tone aligned; internal links per brief.
**Timeline:** Set expectations up front (e.g. 5 business days per round, max 2 rounds). Return feedback in one batch, not drip-fed.
**When blocked:** Legal/PR concerns—ask what's addressable (anonymize, remove metrics, soften claim); escalate with clear ask; don't let draft sit without a path to yes or no.
Pre-Publish Checklist
Run this before publishing any article. Ties directly to Section 3 conversion strategy.

- CTA placement matches brief (after intro, mid-content, after proof, end).

- CTA copy is varied and contextual (not the same line 4 times).

- Product positioning matches keyword tier (aggressive for Tier 1/2, balanced for Tier 3).

- Proof elements from brief are present (case study, quote, metric, comparison).

- Internal links per brief (to related articles, product/comparison pages).

- Meta description and target keyword in place; title optimized for click and keyword.

- Intro addresses searcher problem; objections addressed before final CTA.
If any item is missing, fix before publish. This keeps conversion quality consistent and avoids "publish and forget."
Cadences & Rituals
Build a rhythm so strategy, production, and learning stay aligned.
Weekly:

- Content calendar review: what's publishing, what briefs are due, what's blocked.

- Brief assignments: assign next batch of briefs to writers; confirm proof assets available.

- Social: batch create/schedule posts; review reply backlog.
Monthly:

- Conversion review: which articles drove conversions (by tier); which underperformed; adjust CTA or positioning for underperformers.

- Keyword backlog: reprioritize based on performance and capacity; add new keywords from customer/sales input.

- Case study pipeline: outreach status, interviews scheduled, drafts in review; ensure completed case studies are in proof library for briefs.
Quarterly:

- Goals and strategy (Section 1): are objectives on track? Update baseline and targets if needed.

- Content mix (Section 5): adjust % by type if engagement or conversions warrant.

- Cluster phasing (Section 2): are you on plan (category → competitor → JTBD)? Double down on highest-converting clusters.
Case Study & Social Workflows
**Case study pipeline:**

- Owner: strategist or dedicated PM. Outreach per Section 4 (tiers, timing, templates, incentives).

- Pipeline review monthly: how many in outreach, interview scheduled, draft in progress, approval pending. Target 3x the case studies you need per quarter.

- Handoff: after interview, strategist or writer turns notes into draft using Section 4 template; same review/approval as blog (strategist, then customer approval).

- When published: add to proof asset library with tags (use case, competitor replaced, key metrics). Briefs can then reference "Case study: [Customer] — 70% cost reduction" for Tier 2 comparison content.
**Social repurposing:**

- Trigger: every new Tier 1/2 article and every new case study → 1 thread (pain point → capability → vision of use) + 3–5 single posts or clips. Owner: social owner or writer; social owner schedules and engages.

- Batching: weekly or biweekly batch of social content from recent blog + case studies; align to content mix % and per-stage plan (Section 5).

- Engagement: one person (or rotation) owns reply SLA and proactive commenting; escalate complaints or PR risk per Section 5 playbook.
